KlangFeder

Verse that sounds right while you write it.

For poems and songs: KlangFeder shows you while you write whether your lines sound right – stress, metre, rhyme. And it finds rhymes and words that fit. It speaks German, English and Russian, runs on your own computer and is free.

Write

You see whether the stress is right.

Every syllable shows its stress, every line its metre. If a line stumbles, you see it before anyone sings or reads it aloud.

Stress and metre

Bright means stressed.

Stressed syllables light up, unstressed ones step back. In front of each line its stresses appear as bars; on the right you see their count and the rhyme.

  • German, English and Russian may even be mixed in one text; KlangFeder detects the language line by line.
  • The pronunciation dictionaries know over 740,000 German, 228,000 English and 3 million Russian word forms.
  • The syllable count stands on the left; the beats and the line ending stand on the right (s = stressed ending, u = unstressed ending).
  • You see as much as you want: small switches turn the diagram, the flow and every sound chain on and off – each one where it acts.
  • KlangFeder knows the language of poetry: turn’d, heav’n, o’er and e’er are read with their real number of syllables.

Metre

Iamb, blank verse, sonnet – recognised.

From the beats in each line KlangFeder reads the foot, the line form and the stanza form, following classical prosody.

  • Poems are measured strictly, songs freely: in a song, two unstressed syllables in a row or an upbeat are not a fault.
  • The forms catalogue in the help shows every form as a picture of stressed and unstressed syllables.

Flow

Where the flow stalls.

The stress diagram in front of each line also shows the flow: a gap stands for a pause, a hook for a drop line, and an arrow marks where the sentence runs on into the next line.

  • A drop line is clearly shorter than the other lines of its section.
  • Enjambment is found where a line ends without punctuation and the next one goes on in lower case.
  • Stress density in the tooltip gives the stresses per syllable: a high density feels packed, a low one flowing.
  • The picture shows “The Raven” by Edgar Allan Poe (1845).

Rhyme

Same colour, same rhyme.

KlangFeder recognises rhymes by their sound, not by their spelling. And it finds new ones.

Rhyme scheme

Every section knows its scheme.

Coloured letters show which lines rhyme. Beside each heading stands the scheme: recognised in the text or set by you.

  • For stanzas of four, six or eight lines you can choose alternate rhyme ABAB, couplets AABB, tail rhyme AABCCB or ottava rima ABABABCC; any scheme can also be typed.
  • “auto” takes the scheme that best fits sections of this kind.

Rhyme search

Right-click or search – and the rhymes are there.

The workbench lists the rhymes for a word, level by level, with how often each has been used. Words in bold fit the metre. The search box at the top also finds invented strings of letters or phonetic symbols.

  • The workbench also offers replacement words with the same stress pattern, broader and narrower terms, related words, opposites, phrases – and a translation when the word is in a different language than the interface.
  • The battery beside each word shows how well it fits this very place in the line; full means it matches the beats of that spot exactly.
  • How often a pair of words has been used as a rhyme was counted in about 450,000 German, 790,000 English and 1.1 million Russian rhymes.
  • The search box marks the letters everywhere in the text as you type; “Search” reads an invented word like a known one with the same ending. Next to the word stands its phonetic spelling, and a phonetic keyboard gives an example word for every symbol.

Rhyme levels

Seven levels, each in its colour.

KlangFeder works out from the pronunciation how well two words rhyme. A coloured stripe stands beside each list of rhymes. The first three groups and the last one really rhyme in English verse; the three in between never occur there as rhymes – they are called neighbourhoods:

  • Impure rhymes by the rules of prosody (again / pain, blood / good) count in the rhyme scheme. KlangFeder also recognises assonance between related vowels (German Nebel / Mädel).
  • The list of rhymes starts with perfect rhymes and runs to a mere echo; identical rhymes come last. The mouse wheel decides how far.

near-perfect

gone / on · the two vowels have merged (cot–caught)

identical

breast / rest · even the sound before the stressed vowel is the same

Sound chains

Sound that runs through the verse.

Six switches above the sheet show what sounds together – each with its own marking; the same colour describes one chain. Switch on only what you need right now.

  • Line openings: lines that begin alike or whose first words rhyme appear in the colour of their chain.
  • Internal rhymes are underlined; two neighbouring words that rhyme are joined by a tie, as in music.
  • A multisyllabic rhyme (multi) – the same run of vowels over three or more syllables – is highlighted.
  • Root rhyme is shown as a line above the word, alliteration as a dot before it and assonance as a wave above the vowel.
  • The picture shows “The Raven” by Edgar Allan Poe (1845).

Mondegreens

Two hearings, one sound in between.

Sung, the boundary between two words blurs: “an aim” sounds like “a name”, “I scream” like “ice cream”. Such places can lead to misunderstandings. KlangFeder marks them with two yellow brackets – one hearing above, the other below; the sound under both moves across.

  • What gets reported is what research on word boundaries leads one to expect: a final consonant moves onto a word that begins with a vowel, or the boundary moves before a stressed syllable (Cutler & Butterfield 1992). A small word is always involved – a preposition, pronoun or article.
  • Both hearings appear side by side when you point at the small bracket on the right; the workbench names them at the word.

Grammatical rhyme

Does only the ending rhyme?

In Russian – and really only there – two words often rhyme only through the same inflectional ending – любил / ходил, столом / окном. Prosody calls this a weak rhyme. KlangFeder dots such rhymes and says why; whether you keep them is up to you.

  • Two cases: two verbs in the same form, or the same stressed case ending. If the stem rhymes too – день / тень – it does not count.
  • In the workbench rhyme words in the same form appear as a group of their own after the others. The basis is almost 900,000 inflected forms from Wiktionary.

Find

The right word, in the right metre.

Synonyms, opposites (antonyms) and phrases – each with a sign of how well it fits exactly the place you are looking at. KlangFeder even fills gaps.

Replacements

Same meaning,
same stress.

Synonyms with the same syllable pattern come first. A small battery shows for every suggestion how well it fits exactly this place in the line; at the top stands the line with its beats, the selection marked.

  • A full battery means exactly the stress pattern of this spot. Three quarters mean one syllable more or fewer, half means it stumbles, a quarter stands for a stressed syllable in an unstressed position, and empty means the line gets a different number of beats.
  • English suggestions come from the Open English WordNet; German ones from OpenThesaurus and Wiktionary – with broader and narrower terms and related words, technical terms last as a group of their own; Russian ones from the Russian Wiktionary.
  • “rhymes” marks synonyms that also rhyme with the partner line.
  • Matching the part of speech (German): if a word can be a verb or an adjective – weiß in “ich weiß” (I know) or “ist weiß” (is white) – KlangFeder guesses from the context; one click switches.
  • Idioms and proverbs containing the word appear in groups of their own – for German, over 10,000 idioms and 760 proverbs from the German Wiktionary.

Gaps

Three strokes, and the words fit.

Write ___ or a pattern like x-X-x-X where a word is missing. A right-click shows words in exactly that metre – at the end of a line, rhyming words too.

  • The metre comes from the partner line, the rhyme from the rhyme scheme.
  • In placeholders such as x-X-x-X, lower case means unstressed and capitals stressed; a rhyme sound may end it, as in x-x-IGHT (night, light).
  • One click puts the suggestion in place of the gap.

Sound colour

Words with the right sound.

Hissing, hard, soft, rough, dark or bright: pick one or more sound colours, and in every list of results the matching words move to the front. A line under each word shows how well it matches.

  • Hissing means fricatives and sibilants (s, sh, f), as in a whisper or a rustle. Hard means the plosives (p, t, k) and, at half weight, the glottal stop before vowels. Soft means m, n and l, rough the r, dark u and o, bright i and e.
  • In stage German every r is rolled – also in Herz and Schmerz – and then counts as rough.
  • Nothing is dropped: sound colour sorts and shows; it doesn’t filter.

Phonetic script

Search by sound.

Type a word, part of a word or phonetic script between slashes into the workbench search: /naɪt/ finds rhymes on the sound – invented words too. The symbols sit on an on-screen keyboard for English, German and Russian; point at a key and an example word with exactly that sound appears.

  • The search box also highlights the typed letter sequence everywhere in the text.
  • Right-click a word in the lyrics and the workbench looks that word up.

Root rhymes and word family

Related in sound, related in meaning.

For every word the workbench lists root rhymes – words with the same stressed syllable and a different ending: fire, fireplace, firefly.

  • Root rhymes are found by sound, so words that only sound related appear as well.
  • Word families come from Wiktionary, for German and Russian.

Onset

Same start, same stave.

Switch on “Onset” and type a sound or a word: in every list of results the words whose stressed syllable starts with it move forward – including similar sounds such as t and d or k and g.

  • What counts is the onset of the stressed syllable – that is what carries alliteration.
  • The sound keyboard writes into the field as well; vowels alliterate with each other.

First the form, then the words.

Song or poem.

One switch chooses between song forms and classical poem forms. Every form comes into the sheet with its lines, beats and rhyme scheme.

  • For songs there are pop with pre-chorus, verse–chorus–bridge, AABA, ballad, blues, rap and more – or the structure of your models.
  • For poems there are the sonnet, the Shakespearean sonnet, rhyme royal, the ballad stanza, heroic couplets, the limerick, terza rima, the ghazal, ode stanzas and the haiku.
  • In the structure panel every section can be changed: lines, beats, rhyme, line ending.

loose ↔ strict

Ten lights show how strictly a text is built.

Top centre, ten lights sit between “loose” and “strict”. They weigh four things equally: how pure the rhymes are, how closely the rhyme scheme is kept, whether the number of stressed syllables follows a fixed pattern, and how evenly stressed and unstressed syllables alternate.

  • No right, no wrong. A folk song is strictly built, a spoken verse is loose – both on purpose. The lights only say where your text stands right now.
  • Where you came from: the light with the thin frame marks the starting point – the original or the first version, in the comparison the left-hand text. Once the text has moved, the number appears on the side it moved towards: “stricter +3” on the right, “−2 looser” on the left.
  • This is a display, not a dial. There is nothing to drag here; the lights follow the text, not the other way round.

For Suno

Ready to paste.

If you write for Suno, the service that turns lyrics and a style into a song, KlangFeder lays out text and style the way Suno wants them – counted and ready to copy.

Character count

You see whether it fits.

Lyrics and styles are counted separately against Suno’s limits of 5,000 and 1,000 characters. One button copies each field.

  • The style prompt is saved with every version of your text.
  • Together with KlangTresor, KlangFeder also estimates the length of the song and how densely Suno will sing it.

Directions

[Brackets] are for Suno.

What stands in square brackets is direction: sections, mood, instruments. KlangFeder reads it as structure and leaves it out of the metre.

  • Sections such as [Verse], [Chorus], [Bridge] structure the text; rhyme scheme and metre apply per section.
  • # at the start of a line is a note for you and does not go to Suno.
  • Lines before the first bracket count as a preamble (hints, thanks) and are not measured.

Under the hood

Rhymes, analysed with the methods of bioinformatics.

As biology compares protein and DNA sequences, KlangFeder compares chains of sounds: aligned like BLAST, the search tool for genes and proteins, scored with a substitution matrix learned from rhymes.

How it finds rhymes

Rhymes aligned like genes.

Every word becomes a chain of sounds and is aligned from its end, sound against sound. Three real alignments from KlangFeder: the same sounds give full points, similar ones fewer, and what comes before the rhyme does not count.

  • The alignment is anchored at the end and open to the left (as in BLAST): it keeps growing for as long as it keeps scoring.
  • A table of sound pairs (a substitution matrix, as BLOSUM is for proteins) gives points to sound pairs that meet at a rhyme more often than chance would predict.
  • Syllables have weight: a missing unstressed syllable costs little, a stressed one a lot – which is why light does not rhyme with lighthouse.
  • You choose what rhymes are judged by: poems, songs, both, the sound of the phonemes alone – or your own ear. A short listening quiz (“My rhymes …”) asks which word rhymes better and builds your own sound table from the answers.

My sounds

Your ear decides.

A short listening quiz: one word, two rhyme words – which rhymes better? It only asks where poems and phonetics disagree. Your answers build your own sound table, and the rhyme search works with your ear.

  • The quiz runs page by page and can be stopped at any time: × puts it aside; next time it continues where you left off.
  • A few answers are enough: the starting point is the table learned from poems and songs; your answers only shift the sound pairs you were asked about.
  • You can recalibrate on the System page whenever you like. This is available for German so far.

Sound matrix

Which vowels rhyme.

The square answers the same question in two ways. Lower left shows what poets and songwriters really do: how often two vowels meet at a rhyme, counted in 1,187 books of English verse and 65,000 song lyrics. Upper right shows how alike the two sounds are, judged only by how the mouth forms them, without a single poem. Red means the same on both sides: fits a rhyme, blue: doesn't. The darkest blue marks pairs that never once met at a rhyme – as far apart as it gets. In English almost the whole lower half is that colour, and that is the finding, not a gap in the data.

  • Why the lower half is almost empty: English rhymes nearly always use the very same vowel, so most pairs never met at a rhyme at all. Counted over three million lines of verse and 65,000 song lyrics, the learned table kept shrinking to a handful of pairs, and measured against 1,700 unclean rhymes it had never seen it did worse than the right-hand half. The program therefore goes by that one.
  • What the calculation compares is how the two sounds are made: where the tongue sits, what the lips do, how long the vowel is. That works for every pair, including the ones no poem ever put together. How much each feature counts was tuned on 70 rhyme judgements in German – place in the mouth most, lip rounding least. Before that every feature counted alike, and the upper half had measurably nothing to do with the lower one.
  • What English does learn is the other end of the rhyme: what it costs when a consonant at the end of a word is missing – fire / higher, only / lonely. That alone lifts "the rhyme beats a mere assonance (only the vowels sound the same)" from 96.4 to 96.9 per cent, measured against 5,032 rhyme pairs it had never seen, and costs nothing anywhere else.
  • Eight vowel pairs pass as an almost pure rhyme in English verse: fill / feel, good / food, cup / cop, caught / cot, bed / bay, go / law, cup / book, and marry / merry before an r. In two of them the vowels really have merged for many speakers – caught / cot and marry / merry; the others simply sit close enough together.

Sound tree

Two orders of the same sounds.

The same sounds, ordered twice. On the left they hang together the way verse and song really rhyme them; on the right the way the mouth forms them. The further out two branches meet, the less alike the sounds. The lines in the middle join the same sound on both sides – where they run straight, both orders agree. In English they rarely do: the weights behind the right-hand tree were tuned on German rhyme judgements, and English builds its half-rhymes differently. On the outside of each tree every sound gets a word you can hear it in: the letters carrying the sound are bold, and beside them the other spellings, the commonest first.

  • Until 15 November 2026 almost every line here crossed. No German pair of sounds had the same neighbour in both trees, and between the two orders there was measurably no connection at all. The cause sat in the arithmetic: it counted 22 sound features as equally important, lip rounding among them. For rhyme that is the cheapest difference there is – German has rhymed Liebe / trübe for as long as it has had poetry.
  • Where the new weights come from: 70 judgements by Caspar_D. One word and two rhyme words with the same final consonants, so that only the vowel decides. The same pairs of sounds came back with different words; six of them came out inconsistent and were left out, because there the word decides rather than the sound. The result: place in the mouth weighs most, tongue height almost as much, lip rounding a sixth of that.
  • English keeps its distance. Those weights were tuned on German ears, and English half-rhymes work differently – here the two orders share no neighbour at all. That is itself a finding: how alike two vowels sound for rhyming purposes is not the same question in every language.
  • What no tree can show: a tree forces every sound into exactly one branch. The matrix beside it also shows the pairs that lie across those branches – there every pair stands on its own.

Look

The song as a picture.

A tab of its own shows the text as a word cloud, as a dot plot and as a circle of its repetitions.

  • In the word cloud the area of each word matches its weight; the words move as close together as they can.
  • The dot plot sets the text against itself, syllable by syllable in phonetic script; repetitions become diagonals, as when two DNA sequences are compared.
  • In the circle the song runs clockwise; bands join passages that sound alike – like a Circos plot in genome research.

If you like

Good on its own.
Better together.

KlangFeder works entirely on its own. Switched on, an AI writes with you, and KlangTresor brings your Suno archive as a yardstick.

With AI

Claude or ChatGPT write with you.

The AI proposes storylines, drafts a text in the style of your models or reworks lines – all through your own Anthropic or OpenAI account.

  • Your own account: KlangFeder uses Claude Code or Codex on your computer; the System page helps you sign in.
  • The AI treats your rhyme, beat and structure settings as rules.
  • When the AI is switched off, everything to do with it is hidden, not greyed out.

With KlangTresor

Your archive as the yardstick.

If KlangTresor is running, KlangFeder knows your songs: it estimates how long Suno will take to sing a text, and how densely, judged against your archive.

  • Models: Songs from KlangTresor that you tick as models show their style – metre, rhyme, structure – and serve the AI as examples.
  • The length is only a rough estimate (±12% on average); density says more.
  • KlangTresor is a separate free program for your Suno archive: github.com/CasparDavi/klangtresor.

AI traffic

You see what went to the AI.

A tab of its own shows every request and every reply in full – what KlangFeder sent and what came back. If in doubt, you can read exactly which details left your computer.

  • It stays with you: the record lives in KlangFeder’s own folder, not online. Remove KlangFeder and it goes too.
  • The record is kept per song: the last hundred entries of each song remain, across sessions – so the traffic of the song you worked on yesterday is still there.

Your own reference works

More to find when you search for a word.

KlangFeder also uses reference works that are already on your computer. The System page lists them, each with a tick box and a note on what it adds. On a Mac that includes the Duden in the Dictionary app with typical collocations (fog is dense, grey, it drifts), Ozhegov for Russian meanings and the bilingual dictionaries for translations.

  • The decision is yours. KlangFeder brings no third-party works with it and passes none on; it only asks what is already on your computer.

Sources

Old dictionaries and free corpora, read anew.

KlangFeder learns pronunciation, rhymes and phrases from openly licensed dictionaries and text collections (corpora). Where those do not know a word – or miss its secondary stress – two public-domain pronunciation dictionaries step in, evaluated for it.

Muret-Sanders 1900 · Viëtor 1921

Stress as it was printed.

Muret-Sanders marks each syllable with a curve or a dash to show main and secondary stress; Viëtor spells every word in phonetic script. That is where the stress KlangFeder works with comes from.

  • What for? Rare and old words, compounds and names get their stress from a dictionary instead of a rule – Unwert, Urkraft, pianissimo.
  • English too: the English–German part adds over 100,000 words the American dictionary (CMUdict) does not know – with their stress and rhyme sound.
  • Viëtor’s data is published openly: github.com/CasparDavi/vietor-1921.
  • Both works are in the public domain; the scans come from the Internet Archive.
  • Optionally, KlangFeder stresses words as on the stage around 1900 – German ungesagt instead of ungesagt, English accessory instead of accessory.

System requirements. About 1.5 GB free for program and dictionaries. A current web browser. Other Linux systems: the AppImage. Not signed: on Windows, Edge first holds the download back – click the arrow next to “Delete” → “Keep”; on first launch Windows may ask once more, and macOS blocks the app until you allow it under Privacy & Security → “Open Anyway”. Once installed, a bell next to “System” shows a new version; one click installs it.

Good to know

  • KlangFeder suggests; it does not decide. Stress, metre and rhymes are calculated automatically and can be wrong; the judgement stays with you.
  • Your texts stay on your computer. Only if you let an AI write or revise do your text and request go to Claude or ChatGPT, via your own account there.
